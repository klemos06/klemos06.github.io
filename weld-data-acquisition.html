Weld data acquisition

A weld-monitoring platform that records audio and current. I worked on the acquisition PCBs, embedded software, and the tools used to log and recover the data.

My workPCB design & routing; embedded acquisition software
Tools & hardwareKiCad · C · Linux · SPI · CAN · SQLite

The system

The microphone and current sensor feed a system-on-module running Cortex-M7 firmware and Linux on an A53. I developed the acquisition pipeline, recording workflow, fault checks, and operator display.

Microphone & sensorWeld audio and CAN current measurements
M7 / Linux A53ADC buffering, RPMsg transfer, and SocketCAN
Recorded dataSQLite sessions and operator feedback

The audio path runs at 51.2 kHz. Samples are buffered on the M7 and passed to Linux over RPMsg, where they are stored alongside current measurements.

Stage 1 — Peripheral power & interfaces

I designed the first board to connect sensors, a display, and switches to the system-on-module. It converts 12 V into 5 V, 3.3 V, and 1.8 V supplies, with CAN and AC-coupled microphone connections.

Stage 2 — Backup power

I added an LTC3350 supply and four supercapacitors so the system could save recordings and shut down after losing input power. Power-failure and capacitor-status signals let the host monitor the supply.

Stage 3 — Integrated acquisition

I designed and routed this 6-layer board to bring the IEPE microphone input, AD7768-1 24-bit ADC, CAN interface, and backup supply onto one PCB. The ADC sends samples to the host over SPI.

Software around the hardware

  • Current-triggered weld segmentation with sample-based audio alignment.
  • Monitoring for microphone disconnection, ADC/DRDY failures, CAN faults, and storage problems.
  • UPS-aware shutdown, USB recovery, and database rollover before the FAT32 file-size limit.
  • An HDMI interface showing acquisition state, weld ID, and system faults.
  • A Raspberry Pi-to-AWS pipeline that transferred more than 8 GB of recordings while acquisition continued.

I also operated and monitored the wider system for approximately 48 hours at 140 °F to look for field-relevant failure modes. That test describes the full DAQ system, rather than a separate validation result for each PCB revision.
